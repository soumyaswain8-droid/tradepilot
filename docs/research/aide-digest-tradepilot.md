## What This Is

An Instagram carousel from @the_coding_wizard packaging ten LLM prompts for equity research, each framed as a named institution's analyst persona: Goldman (screening), Morgan Stanley (DCF), Bridgewater (risk), JPMorgan (earnings), BlackRock (portfolio construction), Citadel (technical analysis), Harvard Endowment (dividend income), Bain (competitive moat), RenTech (pattern finding), McKinsey (macro impact).

## The Actual Technique

Strip the brand names and what remains is a reusable prompt architecture, visible in every one of the ten:

1. **Role assignment with seniority and mandate** — "VP-level investment banker who builds valuation models for Fortune 500 M&A deals," not "you are a financial analyst."
2. **A fixed 8–10 item output checklist** — the analyst's deliverable, decomposed. This is what prevents the model from writing a vague essay.
3. **A named output format** — "investment banking valuation memo," "technical analysis report card," "Bain-style competitive strategy deck summary."
4. **A single bracketed input slot** at the end — `[ENTER TICKER SYMBOL]`, `[LIST YOUR PORTFOLIO...]`. The prompt is a template with one variable.
5. **A forced verdict line** — "undervalued / fairly valued / overvalued," "strong buy → strong sell," "my single best stock pick." Non-optional commitment.

## What Is Actually Useful vs. Not

**Useful:** the checklist + format + forced-verdict structure. It converts an open-ended question into a filled-in template, which is why the outputs read as rigorous.

**Not useful:** the institution names carry no capability. An LLM does not have Citadel's data or RenTech's models. Worse, prompts 1, 6, 7 and 9 ask the model to produce *specific numbers* — exact support levels, current dividend yields, RSI readings, historical anomalies — which a model without a live market data feed will hallucinate confidently. The post's own disclaimer ("AI will not magically predict the market") is doing real work.

**The dividing line:** prompts that structure *reasoning over supplied data* (2, 3, 8, 10) are sound. Prompts that ask the model to *retrieve* market data (1, 6, 7, 9) are unsafe unless wired to a real data source.

**How to use:** The transferable asset here is the five-part prompt skeleton (senior role + mandate → 8-10 item output checklist → named artifact format → single bracketed input slot → forced verdict), not the finance content. For TradePilot, prompts 3, 8 and 10 map directly onto agent roles you do not yet have: a macro-regime classifier and a risk/exposure stress-tester that runs over your own positions rather than inventing data — and the forced-verdict line is exactly the discipline TradePilot's root-cause note points at (edge is execution discipline, not ML). Beyond trading, the same skeleton is a template factory for the Surya AI deliverable line: the Bain competitive-analysis prompt (#8) is a near drop-in for the funding and market-research reports in aide/deliverables, and the McKinsey macro prompt (#10) is a reusable shape for any sector brief. ## What It Is
An Instagram post from @seb.ai showcasing a continuously running AI trading agent built with Claude (Fable 5). It is positioned explicitly as a research and decision-support system — human review required, not autonomous execution.

## The Workflow
The system runs a six-stage pipeline: **SCAN → SIGNALS → PLAN → RISK → MONITOR → DECISION**.

- **Inputs monitored:** price action, news, volume, trends, and watchlists.
- **Trade plan output:** entry, target, stop, invalidation condition, and risk level — a complete, falsifiable plan per setup.
- **Risk gate:** before any plan advances, a separate check validates position size, total exposure, drawdown, volatility, and maximum loss.
- **Terminal states:** every setup resolves to one of three verdicts — APPROVED, WATCHLIST, or REJECTED.

## Why the Architecture Matters
Two design choices stand out beyond the hype framing:

1. **Risk as a separate gate, not a prompt suggestion.** The risk system sits *between* plan generation and decision, so a good-looking setup can still be rejected on exposure or drawdown grounds. This separation of signal generation from risk approval mirrors how institutional desks work.
2. **Three-state output instead of binary.** The WATCHLIST middle state lets the agent defer without forcing a trade/no-trade call — reducing overtrading pressure, which is the classic failure mode of automated systems.

## Caveats
This is engagement-bait content (comment "24/7" for the prompts), so the actual implementation depth is unverified. No performance data, no backtest, no cost figures. Treat it as an architecture sketch, not a validated system. The disclaimer (human review required) is doing significant load-bearing work.

## Credibility
Medium-low as a system; medium-high as a pattern reference. The pipeline shape is sound and matches known agentic best practice, but nothing here is evidenced.

**How to use:** This maps almost one-to-one onto TradePilot, where the root-cause finding was that the edge is execution discipline, not ML — and this post's architecture is pure execution discipline: a hard risk gate between plan and decision, and a three-state verdict (APPROVED/WATCHLIST/REJECTED) that structurally suppresses overtrading. The v8 April-recipe engine could adopt the explicit invalidation-condition field in every trade plan (a falsifiable exit trigger, distinct from the stop) and the separated risk-approval stage as formal pipeline steps rather than inline checks. ## What it is
Kronos is an open-source foundation model from a Tsinghua research team (lead author Yu Shi), trained specifically on financial candlestick data rather than adapted from a general time-series or language model. It ingests raw OHLCV sequences and autoregressively generates future candles — the post cites a 400-in / 120-out setup. Weights are MIT-licensed and published on Hugging Face (`NeoQuasar/Kronos-*`, in mini/small/base sizes) with a small PyTorch inference and fine-tuning repo. The post claims AAAI 2026 acceptance; treat that and the "45+ exchanges" figure as author-reported until checked against the AAAI entry.

## Why it's architecturally interesting
The hard part of applying transformers to price data is that candles are continuous and multivariate. Kronos handles this by quantizing each candle into discrete tokens (a learned tokenizer) and then running a standard decoder-only LM over that token stream. That means the output is a *distribution* over future paths — you can sample many trajectories, not just a point forecast. For a system that already sizes positions by conviction, sampled path dispersion is a usable uncertainty signal.

## The catch (matches your own findings)
The post's warning is the same lesson TradePilot already learned the hard way: raw predictive signal is not P&L. Kronos optimizes next-candle likelihood, not risk-adjusted return after fees, slippage, and market-neutralization. Backtests of pretrained forecasters typically decay sharply once costs and realistic fills are applied — and a 400-candle context is short enough that regime shifts sit largely outside its window.

## Verdict
Worth one bounded experiment as a *feature source*, not a strategy. Zero-shot equity performance on Indian markets is unknown; fine-tuning on your own NSE data is the only honest test.

**How to use:** Run this as a strictly time-boxed TradePilot side-experiment, not a rewrite. Concretely: pull Kronos-small zero-shot, feed it the same NSE bars v5 already consumes, and score it purely as an additional feature — forecast direction plus the dispersion across sampled paths — rather than as a standalone signal. Then test it exactly the way v5 was validated: alpha t-stat, market-neutral, cost-robust, with the same ship-gate that killed the 5-tree ML twin. Given your own root-cause finding that TradePilot's edge is execution discipline rather than ML sophistication, the null hypothesis should be that Kronos adds nothing net of costs — so pre-commit to dropping it if it fails the gate, the way v8 handled the ML twin. Secondary uses: the sampled-path dispersion is a cheap regime/uncertainty gauge for position sizing even if the directional forecast is useless, and the tokenizer-plus-decoder design is a reference architecture worth noting for Sarathi's brain and for any "agentic trading platform" positioning material, since a credible open-source Chinese foundation model in this space is now part of the competitive story you tell investors (Hub71, funding docs)."}

## The Five Greeks
- **Delta** — how much the option premium moves per ₹1 move in the underlying. Also a rough proxy for probability of expiring in-the-money.
- **Gamma** — the rate of change of Delta itself; highest at-the-money near expiry, which is why ATM options feel 'explosive' on expiry day.
- **Theta** — time decay; the daily erosion of premium, which accelerates in the final week. This is the seller's edge and the buyer's tax.
- **Vega** — sensitivity to implied volatility; premiums can rise or crush on IV shifts (e.g. before/after events like budget day or RBI policy) with no price move at all.
- **Rho** — sensitivity to interest rates; largely negligible for short-dated index options.

## Assessment

## Relevance
For a systematic trading context, the actionable core is: premium P&L decomposes into delta, gamma, theta, and vega components. Any options strategy that is evaluated only on directional (delta) terms is mis-measured — theta and vega attribution are what separate 'the view was right' from 'the trade made money'.

**How to use:** For TradePilot, this is a prompt to add Greeks-aware evaluation if/when the engine touches options: decompose每 trade's P&L into delta/theta/vega attribution so execution-discipline claims (the validated v5 edge) can be tested against premium-decay effects rather than direction alone — and treat vega/IV crush as an explicit risk check around events. ## Indian market translation

NSE runs 09:15–15:30 IST with the same shape: 09:15–10:15 opening drive, a 11:30–14:00 lull, and a 14:30–15:30 close ramp distorted by expiry-day and MTM flows. Thursday/Tuesday expiry sessions break the curve entirely and should be modelled separately.

## The transferable idea

A time-of-day filter is the cheapest possible trade-count reducer — no new signal, no new model, one boolean on the entry gate.

**How to use:** This lands directly on TradePilot's open root-cause question — the finding that the edge is execution discipline rather than model sophistication, and that win rate collapsed from 82% to 48% as trade count rose from 17 to 45. A session-time filter is the single cheapest way to cut trade count without touching the signal: bucket every historical v5/v8 trade by IST entry time into 09:15–10:15, 10:15–11:30, 11:30–14:00, 14:00–15:30, and compute win rate, average R, and slippage per bucket, with expiry days held out.
